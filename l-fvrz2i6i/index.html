Tokens

✓ Got it
Say it like thisTokens are the little chunks of text an AI model actually reads and writes — not always whole words.

Analogy: Like LEGO bricks. The sentence “Chicago trees” might become a few bricks: “Chi”, “cago”, “ trees”. The model builds meaning brick by brick.

Your work: When your AI Visibility Check agent runs searches and writes a score report, every word it reads and writes costs tokens. Shorter prompts = cheaper runs.

Context window

✓ Got it
Say it like thisThe context window is how much text the model can hold in its “working memory” at once — the prompt, the docs you paste, and its reply all share that space.

Analogy: A backpack. Stuff it with a long PDF, chat history, and instructions — eventually things fall out (get truncated) or the trip gets expensive.

Your work: Your job-hunt assistant keeps chat history and job details in context. Pasting a huge resume + 10 job posts can overflow a small window.

Embeddings

✓ Got it
Say it like thisAn embedding turns meaning into a list of numbers so a computer can tell which ideas are close — “roofer” sits near “roofing,” not near “pizza.”

Analogy: Dropping every word on a map. Similar meanings cluster in the same neighborhood.

Your work: If you searched your podcast transcripts for “tools that write emails,” embeddings find episodes about “AI that drafts outreach” even if the exact words differ.

Vector databases

✓ Got it
Say it like thisA vector database stores embeddings and finds the closest matches fast — like a search engine for meaning, not exact keywords.

Analogy: A library sorted by “vibe,” not by alphabet. You ask for “storm damage quotes” and it pulls the nearest shelves.

Your work: For a client site knowledge base (services, FAQs, pricing notes), you’d embed each chunk and store them in a vector DB so the chatbot retrieves the right paragraphs.

RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation)

✓ Got it
Say it like thisRAG means: look up the right documents first, then let the AI answer using those docs — so answers stay grounded in your stuff, not just the model’s memory.

Analogy: An open-book test. The AI doesn’t guess from memory; it flips to the right pages, then writes.

Your work: doesaiknowmybusiness.com is RAG-shaped: the agent searches live Google results (retrieve), then scores and explains with sources (generate). Same idea for a business FAQ bot.

RAG walkthrough

1
Question

“Does Aguilar Roofing show up when people ask AI for a Chicago roofer?”

2
Retrieve

Search the web / your docs. Pull top chunks: Google results, reviews, site status.

3
Stuff the prompt

Put those chunks into the context window with clear instructions.

4
Generate + cite

Model writes a score and fix plan, pointing back at the sources.

MCP (Model Context Protocol)

✓ Got it
Say it like thisMCP is a standard plug so an AI assistant can safely use tools and data sources — Gmail, Calendar, GitHub, Drive — without a custom integration for each app.

Analogy: USB-C for AI. One plug shape, many devices. The brain (the model) stays the same; the tools swap.

Your work: Your Grok bots use connectors (Gmail, Calendar, Drive, GitHub). MCP is the idea behind “the bot can read my inbox and draft a reply” without you pasting everything by hand.

7

Agents & LangChain-style loops

✓ Got it
Say it like thisAn agent is an AI that can plan, use tools, check results, and loop until the job is done — not just answer one prompt.

Analogy: A new hire with a checklist: think → use a tool → look at the result → decide next step → repeat.

Your work: The visibility check agent: plan searches → call Google → read results → score → write fix plan. LangChain (and similar frameworks) help wire that loop; you don’t need to memorize the API — know the pattern.

Agent loop

1. Goal — “Check if this Chicago shop shows up in AI answers.”
2. Plan — Decide which searches / tools to run.
3. Act — Call a tool (web search, site fetch).
4. Observe — Read the tool output.
5. Repeat or finish — More tools, or write the final score + plan.
8

Prompting techniques

✓ Got it
Say it like thisGood prompting is clear instructions, examples, constraints, and structure — role, goal, format, and what “good” looks like.

Analogy: Briefing a contractor. “Fix the roof” fails. “Replace damaged shingles on the south slope, match color, text photos when done” works.

Your work: Outreach texts, site copy, and your bots all run better with role + constraints (“friendly Chicago tone, under 320 chars, no fake metrics”).

Technique cheat sheet

No invented reviews. Spanish option. Tap-to-call CTA.

F
Format

“Return only the SMS text, no preamble.”

Also know: chain-of-thought (“think step by step” for reasoning), and tool-calling prompts that list available tools.

Fine-tuning vs RAG

✓ Got it
Say it like thisRAG pulls fresh facts into the prompt. Fine-tuning changes the model’s behavior or style by training on examples. Most business Q&A needs RAG first; fine-tune when you need a consistent voice or skill.

Analogy: RAG = open book on the desk. Fine-tune = sending the employee to a training class so they “just know” the house style.

Your work: Client FAQs and prices change weekly → RAG. Making every reply sound like Nick’s outreach voice → maybe a small fine-tune or strong few-shot prompts (cheaper to start with prompts + RAG).

Hallucinations

✓ Got it
Say it like thisA hallucination is when the model says something confident that isn’t true — made-up citations, fake metrics, wrong phone numbers.

Analogy: A smooth talker who fills gaps instead of saying “I don’t know.”

Your work: You never invent Google review counts in outreach. Same rule for AI: ground answers in search/RAG, ask for sources, and refuse to fabricate.

How you’d reduce them

4
Validate

Your work: Scoring AI visibility or writing a legal-ish claim email → low temp. Brainstorming Roblox game ideas → higher temp.

Evals

✓ Got it
Say it like thisEvals are tests for AI systems — a set of example inputs with expected quality — so you know a prompt or model change helped, not just “felt better.”

Analogy: A practice exam before the real interview. Same questions, score the answers.

Your work: For outreach, keep 20 sample businesses and grade drafts: length, no fake metrics, clear CTA. For the visibility app, spot-check scores against what you see in ChatGPT/Perplexity.

Guardrails & AI safety

✓ Got it
Say it like thisGuardrails are rules and filters that keep AI from doing harmful, private, or off-brand things — before and after the model replies.

Analogy: Hard hats and “do not cut” tags on a job site. The crew can still work; unsafe moves get blocked.

Your work: Kid-facing ideas (howaiworksforkids / book) need no ads, no data collection from kids, age-appropriate content. Business bots: don’t send email without approval; don’t invent credentials.

15

Cost & latency

✓ Got it
Say it like thisCost is mostly tokens in + tokens out (and tool calls). Latency is how long the user waits. Bigger models and longer context usually cost more and run slower.

Analogy: Taking the big bucket truck for a tiny branch — works, but slow and expensive. Match the tool to the job.

Your work: Visibility checks that run many searches add tool latency. Cache repeats, shorten prompts, use a small model for classify / big model for final write-up.

Tradeoff knobs

↓Shorter prompts
↓Smaller model
↓Cache / reuse

Interview line: “I’d measure dollars per successful task and p95 response time, then cut context and model size until quality dips.”

1. Tell me about yourself
I’m Nick Castro — Chicago-based. By day I work as a City tree trimmer; on the side I’ve been teaching myself AI by shipping real tools. I built doesaiknowmybusiness.com, a free AI Visibility Check that shows whether AI search recommends a local business. I build mobile sites for Chicago shops (like aaproroofers.com), I run a short podcast called AI Tools Pulse, and I made robloxideas.com plus a kids’ book on machine learning with my wife Nancy. I hold Google IT certificates. I’m looking for soft AI roles — enablement, automation, specialist, trainer — where I can help non-technical people actually use AI.
2. Tell me about an AI project (STAR) — doesaiknowmybusiness.com
Situation: Local businesses get reviews on Google but often don’t show up when people ask ChatGPT or Perplexity who to hire. Task: I wanted a free tool that checks AI visibility and gives a fix plan in plain English (and Spanish). Action: I shipped doesaiknowmybusiness.com — an agent runs live searches, scores 0–100, lists competitors AI names instead, checks the website, and outputs a fix plan with ready-to-paste schema. Result: It’s a live product I can demo in interviews. It shows I understand agents, retrieval, grounding answers in real data, and packaging AI for small businesses — not just chatting with ChatGPT.
3. What is RAG and when would you use it?
RAG means retrieve relevant documents first, then generate an answer from them. I’d use it anytime answers must stay tied to company docs, prices, or live search — like a business FAQ bot or my visibility checker pulling real Google results before scoring. It’s usually better than fine-tuning when facts change often.
4. Tokens vs context window?
Tokens are the chunks the model reads and writes — billing and length limits are in tokens. The context window is the max tokens the model can see at once: system prompt, user message, retrieved docs, and the reply all share that backpack. Overflow means truncation or higher cost.
5. What are embeddings / vector databases?
Embeddings turn text into numbers that capture meaning so “storm damage roof” sits near “emergency roof repair.” A vector database stores those and finds nearest neighbors fast. That’s the retrieval engine behind most RAG chatbots.
6. Fine-tuning vs RAG?
RAG for fresh facts and citations. Fine-tuning to change style or teach a skill the model should “just have.” I’d start with good prompts + RAG; only fine-tune if we still can’t get consistent tone or behavior after that.
7. How do you reduce hallucinations?
Ground with tools or RAG, require citations, lower temperature on factual tasks, and validate numbers and URLs. In my outreach I refuse fake review counts — same discipline with AI outputs. For sends, I keep a human approval step.
8. What is temperature?
A randomness knob. Near 0 for scoring, claims, or anything that must be consistent. Higher for brainstorming Roblox ideas or podcast hooks. I’d set it per task, not one global value.
9. What is MCP?
Model Context Protocol — a standard way for an assistant to connect to tools and data (Gmail, Drive, GitHub, etc.). Like USB-C for AI apps. It matters for enablement because teams can plug systems in without a custom integration every time.
10. What is an AI agent?
An AI that loops: plan, call tools, observe results, repeat until done. My visibility app is agent-shaped — it doesn’t one-shot a paragraph; it searches, reads, then writes a scored report. Frameworks like LangChain help wire that pattern; the concept matters more than the brand name.
11. How would you evaluate an AI feature?
Build a small golden set — real examples with a rubric (accuracy, tone, length, no PII leaks). Score before/after prompt changes. For my outreach drafts: under 320 chars, no invented metrics, clear CTA. Pair that with a few live spot-checks.
12. Zapier vs Make vs n8n?
Zapier is the fastest for simple triggers. Make is strong for visual multi-step scenarios. n8n is flexible and friendlier to self-hosting/control. I’d pick based on who maintains it, budget, and whether we need AI steps plus human approval in the loop.
13. How do you think about AI cost and latency?
Cost scales with tokens and tool calls; latency grows with model size and sequential tools. I’d shorten prompts, cache repeats, use a small model for routing and a larger one for final writing, and track cost per successful task plus wait time.
14. How do you teach non-technical people to use AI?
Start from their real job, not the model. Short demos, templates, and guardrails. I already explain AI simply — kids’ book with Nancy, AI Tools Pulse, and sites for tradespeople. Enablement is translating AI into “here’s the workflow you run on Monday.”
15. Why you / why this role?
I ship. I’m not waiting to “finish learning Python” — I build tools people can click. I know Chicago small businesses, I’m bilingual-friendly in how I write, and I live the enablement problem every day: making AI useful for people who aren’t engineers. Soft AI roles are where that skill set lands.
